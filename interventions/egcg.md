## What it is

Green tea (Camellia sinensis) contains catechins, of which epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) is the most-studied. Available as:
- Brewed tea (typical EGCG dose 50–150 mg/cup)
- Standardized green tea extract supplements (often 200–1000 mg EGCG/day)
- Decaffeinated extracts

Glossary note: Chinese 茶多酚 (chá duō fēn) translates literally as "tea polyphenols" and refers to the catechin-rich extract fraction, of which EGCG is the dominant single component (~50–60% of total catechin mass in green-tea extract). Chinese-language papers tagged 茶多酚 are recorded under EGCG with the explicit caveat that the intervention is the broader extract, not isolated EGCG, unless the paper specifies otherwise. ## Proposed mechanism

- Antioxidant / pro-oxidant biphasic effects (low doses anti-, high doses pro-)
- AMPK activation, mTOR modulation
- Effects on gut microbiome, fat absorption
- Modest blood pressure and lipid effects
- Wnt/β-catenin modulation in muscle (zh-011 mechanism claim, single-lab)

**Confidence:** Established for the biochemistry; Plausible for in-vivo translation at dietary doses; uncertain at supplement doses.

## Confounds

- **Hepatotoxicity at supplement doses** changes the calculus — moderate green tea drinking is well-tolerated, high-dose EGCG capsules carry real liver risk (EFSA 2018; FDA case reports).
- **Confounding in observational cohorts** — green tea consumption tracks with East Asian dietary, exercise, and tobacco patterns. Mendelian randomization is limited.
- **Polyphenol class effects** — separating EGCG-specific from broader catechin or polyphenol effects is difficult. Chinese 茶多酚 sources are class-level by construction; treating them as EGCG-specific evidence would be over-claiming.
- **Chinese-language publication-bias signal** — Chinese tea-polyphenol literature is large and skews positive; effect-size estimates may be optimistic.
- **Single-lab rat sarcopenia (zh-011)** — single-lab disease-model surrogate; weakest evidence shape, prone to non-replication.
- **Citation retrieval gap (zh-011)** — primary CNKI record not retrieved this run; do not let this source carry verdict weight.

## Conflict of interest scan

- Supplement industry presence in English-language EGCG-supplementation trials.
- Chinese-cohort tea-polyphenol literature has heterogeneous funding disclosure; assume the standard Chinese-RCT discount.
- zh-011 funding disclosure unverifiable until primary retrieval.
- Net: modest discount on enthusiastic claims; verdict-relevant claims unchanged.

## Human translation

Unchanged from Round 1:
1. **Drinking 2–4 cups of green tea daily:** plausible modest benefit on cardiometabolic markers and possibly mortality based on observational data; safe; cheap; reasonable as dietary practice.
2. **High-dose EGCG supplementation (>500–800 mg/day):** the hepatotoxicity signal is real, the marginal benefit over moderate tea consumption unclear, and the longevity claim unsupported.
3. **EGCG / tea polyphenols for sarcopenia or "muscle aging":** the zh-011 rat result is suggestive at best; not sufficient to recommend supplementation for this purpose.
4. **EGCG for cancer prevention:** mechanism plausible, RCT evidence underwhelming.

## Calibrated verdict

**Suggestive** for green tea consumption (moderate doses). **Mostly hype** with safety caveat for high-dose EGCG supplements.

Compared to **resveratrol (Mostly hype)** — the relevant anchor for "popular polyphenol, lab-paper enthusiasm, weak hard endpoints" — EGCG has *cleaner observational data* (better cohorts, more consistent direction in East Asian populations), which keeps the dietary-form verdict above resveratrol. The supplement-form verdict matches resveratrol's pattern, with the additional hepatotoxicity concern. The zh-011 addition does not change this comparison.

Cross-checked against **Epitalon (Mostly hype)** — the Round 2 multilingual anchor — the comparison is informative: zh-011 has the same evidence shape as the typical Khavinson Epitalon paper (single-lab, surrogate, citation-trail issues), but EGCG's *English-language* base is far stronger. This sweep does not produce a Q3+ Chinese source that would force EGCG up; conversely, if it had, that would have been a flag to revisit Epitalon's anchor too.

Compared to **omega-3 (Suggestive general, Round 1)**, the two are comparable for the dietary form; EGCG supplements are weaker than omega-3 supplements due to the safety signal.

## Confidence interval on verdict

- Tea-consumption verdict stable.
- Supplement-form verdict unlikely to move up given safety profile.
- Most likely 2-year trajectory: stable.
- Verdict would move only with: (a) a pre-registered RCT with hard endpoints showing benefit at supplement doses below the hepatotoxicity threshold, or (b) Mendelian-randomization evidence isolating tea-consumption effects from East Asian lifestyle confounds.

## Open questions

- Q: Does the green tea mortality association survive in carefully matched non-East-Asian cohorts, or is it a regional-lifestyle confound?
- Q: What is the safe ceiling dose of EGCG supplementation, and does any benefit materialize below the hepatotoxicity threshold?
- Q: Are there responder-vs-non-responder genetic variants (e.g., catechol-O-methyltransferase) that meaningfully change the calculus?
- Q (multilingual-specific): Is there a Japanese-language RCT base on tea-catechin supplementation with hard endpoints that English indexing has missed? J-STAGE / ICHUSHI sweep returned nothing this round; flag for re-sweep.
- Q (multilingual-specific): Is the zh-011 Wnt/β-catenin / sarcopenia mechanism replicated in any independent rodent line?
